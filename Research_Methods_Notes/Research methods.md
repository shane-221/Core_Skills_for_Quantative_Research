# Thinking About Research

## What Is Research?
Research is the process we undertake when:

1. **We have a question to answer** (the research question)  
2. **We need to acquire knowledge** (the research process)  
3. **We want to extend our understanding of the world** (the research outcome)



## Key Terms Defined
#### **Theory**
Ideas or concepts used to explain and understand an event, situation, or phenomenon.  
Theories operate at three levels:

- **Macro** – grand theories covering broad aspects of the social world  
- **Meso** – mid‑level theories relating to organisations, families, communities  
- **Micro** – small‑scale, local theories  

#### **Concept**
An abstract idea that describes or encapsulates a phenomenon.

####  **Paradigm**
A clustering of ontological, epistemological, and theoretical positions.

####  **Hypothesis**
A testable assertion about the relationship between things.



## What Makes Good Research?
#### **1. Structure and Purpose**
Provides knowledge that is useful and meaningful.

#### **2. Rigour**
Research is carefully planned and systematically conducted.

#### **3. Robust and Defensible (Quality)**

- **Validity and Credibility**  
  Am I researching the correct phenomenon? Is the data relevant and appropriate for the research question?

- **Generalisability and Transferability**  
  To what extent can the findings be applied beyond the study? In which contexts are the conclusions valid?

#### **4. Systematic**
The research follows a logical and organised process.



## Steps of the Research Process
<div align="center">## Step 1: Research Paradigm</div>


There are four components to consider when establishing a research paradigm:

#### 1. **Domain**
- What is the domain of inquiry?
- What constitutes the subject matter?

#### 2. **Reflexivity**
- How do I relate to the world and the subject matter?
- What assumptions, experiences, or positionalities do I bring?

#### 3. **Ontology (What Exists?)**
Ontology concerns the nature of reality — what kinds of things exist in the world.

Common ontological positions include:

- **Objectivism**  
  Social phenomena exist independently of social actors. They have an objective reality outside of individual interpretation.

- **Constructivism**  
  Social phenomena are real but constructed through human interpretation. Meanings are created, negotiated, and revised by individuals and groups.  
  The researcher brings their own meanings into the study.

- **Realism**  
  Reality exists independently of the researcher, but parts of it are hidden and not directly observable.  
  The goal is to uncover underlying mechanisms that generate observable events.

#### 4. **Epistemology (How Do We Come to Know?)**
Epistemology concerns how knowledge is produced and what counts as valid knowledge.

- **Positivism**  
  Linked to objectivism.  
  Assumes an independent social reality that can be measured.  
  Emphasises quantitative data, causal relationships, and objective observation.

- **Interpretivism**  
  Linked to constructivism.  
  Prioritises subjective meanings, lived experiences, and context.  
  Emphasises qualitative, rich, contextual understanding.

- **Realism**  
  Reality exists independently of the researcher, but is shaped by hidden structures or mechanisms.  
  Research aims to uncover these mechanisms and their observable effects.  
  Can use quantitative or qualitative data.  
  Often used to study power relations or structural forces.

### <center>Step 2: Research Question</center>

#### Step 2.1 Understanding the Type of Research Question
---
There are four main types of research questions:

1. **Descriptive Research**  
   Focuses on describing characteristics, patterns, or phenomena as they exist, without investigating causes.

2. **Exploratory Research**  
   Conducted when little is known about a topic.  
   Aims to explore, identify patterns, and generate initial insights or hypotheses.

3. **Explanatory Research**  
   Seeks to explain why something happens, mainly through identifying and analysing causal relationships.

4. **Evaluation Research**  
   Assesses the effectiveness, impact, or value of a program, policy, intervention, or process.

Some research questions may combine elements of more than one type.

#### Step 2.2 Hypotheses and Their Role
---
The research question often leads to the development of a **hypothesis**.

A hypothesis is used to examine **relationships between concepts** that are **statistically testable**.  
This means:

- Key elements can be identified and measured  
- Data can be gathered  
- Statistical theory can be applied to test the relationship  

There are two main types of relationships typically tested in this way:

1. **Causal** — how two variables move together

2. **Associative relationships** — whether one variable influences or produces change in another

#### Step 2.3 Operational Definitions
---
To refine your research question, define the **key concepts** clearly and consistently.

Operational definitions ensure that:

- Each concept has a precise meaning  
- The meaning is consistent across all stages of testing  
- The data collected aligns with the intended concept  
- Other researchers can understand and replicate your approach  

You may use existing definitions from the literature.  
It is also good practice to **pilot test** your definitions to ensure clarity and consistency.



#### Step 2.4 Developing the Research Question
---
Your initial research question may begin at a **high level**.

As you refine your operational definitions, you may identify **subsidiary questions** that need to be addressed.  
These help you:

- Specify the exact area of focus  
- Break down broad questions into manageable components  
- Ensure alignment between concepts, definitions, and methods  
- Clarify the scope and boundaries of your study


### <center>Step 3: Mitigating considerations</center>


#### 3.1 The Role of Data
---- 
We cannot directly observe or fully “know” social phenomena.  
Instead, **data acts as a proxy** that allows us to infer what is happening.

Key considerations:

1. **Forms of Data**
   - **Spoken and written text**
   - **Non‑verbal communication**
   - **Factual data** — obtained through observation or from knowledgeable sources
   - **Value statements** — expressions of opinions, judgements, or criteria

2. **Structure of Data**
   Consider how the data is organised:
   - Chronologically  
   - By theme or category  
   - By context or setting  
   - By specific components of the phenomenon  

3. **Data as a Construct**
   Data is produced by individuals or institutions.  
   Therefore, researchers must consider:
   - What the data contains  
   - How it was produced  
   - What assumptions or biases may be embedded  

4. **Primary vs. Secondary Data**
   - **Primary data** — created directly by the researcher  
   - **Secondary data** — produced by others  
   Each has strengths and limitations (e.g., control vs. availability).

5. **Data and Research Approaches**
   - **Positivist** → Factual + value‑neutral data → *Quantitative methods*  
   - **Interpretivist** → Language, spoken/written/non‑verbal data → *Qualitative methods*  

   *Mixed‑methods approaches are increasingly common.*


#### 3.2 The Role of Ethics
---
Ethics refers to the **social and moral values** involved in conducting research.

Key considerations:

- **Ethics approvals**  
  Required for many forms of human‑subject research.

- **Informed Consent**  
  - Participation must be voluntary  
  - Participants must understand the purpose, risks, and procedures  
  - Consent must be informed, explicit, and freely given  

- **Power Relationships**  
  Consider imbalances between researcher and participant.

- **Protection from Harm**  
  Researchers must minimise risks, including:
  - Physical harm  
  - Psychological harm  
  - Social or reputational consequences  
  - Breaches of confidentiality  
  - Violations of anonymity  
  - Data protection failures  
  - Cultural insensitivity  

The overarching principle is **protection from harm**.


### <center>Steap 4: Planning the research Project 

When planning your research project, there are two groups of questions to consider:

#### Step 4.1 Questions Related to the Research Project
---
a. **What is the purpose of the research?**  
   - What are you trying to understand, explain, evaluate, or explore?

b. **What research has already been done?**  
   - What does the existing literature say?  
   - Where are the gaps or unresolved issues?

c. **How will you collect, analyse, and present your findings?**  
   - Methods of data collection  
   - Analytical techniques  
   - How results will be communicated

d. **What do you think the outcome of the work will be?**  
   - Expected contributions  
   - Potential implications  
   - How the findings might be used



#### Step 4.2 Practical Considerations
----
a. **Will you work alone or as part of a team?**  
   - Roles, responsibilities, and collaboration structure

b. **What is your timetable?**  
   - Milestones  
   - Deadlines  
   - Time allocation for each stage

c. **How will you measure progress?**  
   - Checkpoints  
   - Criteria for completion  
   - Mechanisms for reviewing and adjusting the plan
