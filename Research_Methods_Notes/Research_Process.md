## 1. What Is Research?

Research is the process we undertake when:

- We have a question to answer (the **research question**)
- We need to acquire knowledge (the **research process**)
- We want to extend our understanding of the world (the **research outcome**)

---

## 2. Key Terms Defined

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Theory** | Ideas or concepts used to explain and understand an event, situation, or phenomenon. Operates at macro (grand), meso (organisational/community), or micro (local/small-scale) levels. |
| **Concept** | An abstract idea that describes or encapsulates a phenomenon. |
| **Paradigm** | A clustering of ontological, epistemological, and theoretical positions. |
| **Hypothesis** | A testable assertion about the relationship between things. |

---

## 3. What Makes Good Research?

Good research is characterised by four qualities:

**1. Structure and Purpose**
Provides knowledge that is useful and meaningful.

**2. Rigour**
Research is carefully planned and systematically conducted.

**3. Robust and Defensible (Quality)**
- *Validity and Credibility:* Am I researching the correct phenomenon? Is the data relevant and appropriate for the research question?
- *Generalisability and Transferability:* To what extent can the findings be applied beyond the study? In which contexts are the conclusions valid?

**4. Systematic**
The research follows a logical and organised process.

## 4. Steps of the Research Process

### Step 1: Research Paradigm

Four things to consider:

1. **Domain** — What is the domain? What constitutes subject matter?
2. **Reflexivity** — How do I relate to the world and the subject matter?
3. **Ontology** — What exists? (Objects, properties, events, processes, relations, etc.)
4. **Epistemology** — How do we come to know?

#### Ontological Positions

| Position | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Objectivism** | Social phenomena exist independently of social actors. |
| **Constructivism** | Social phenomena are constructed and reworked by those involved. Meanings are open to study; researchers bring their own meanings. |
| **Realism** | Part of social reality cannot be directly observed — it is hidden beneath surface phenomena. |

#### Epistemological Positions

| Position | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Positivism** | Stems from objectivism. Social reality is independent of researcher and subjects. Favours quantitative data, measurement, and causal relationships. |
| **Interpretivism** | Connected to constructivism. Studies social phenomena relative to subjects. Prioritises subjective interpretations; typically qualitatively rich. |
| **Realism** | Reality exists outside the researcher and can be studied objectively. But social reality is underpinned by invisible structures or mechanisms — the goal is to identify these hidden mechanisms and their observable effects. Can use quantitative or qualitative data. |

---

### Step 2: Research Questions

#### 2.1 Types of Research Questions

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Descriptive** | Describes characteristics, patterns, or phenomena as they exist — no causal investigation. |
| **Exploratory** | Used when little is known; aims to explore, identify patterns, and generate hypotheses. |
| **Explanatory** | Seeks to explain *why* something happens, through causal analysis. |
| **Evaluation** | Assesses the effectiveness, impact, or value of a programme, policy, or intervention. |

> Some research questions combine elements of more than one type.

Research questions lead to hypotheses — testable assertions about statistically measurable relationships. Two main types:

- **Causal relationships:** A causes B
- **Associative relationships:** A and B are related (direction and mechanism may be unclear)

#### 2.2 Operational Definitions

Define all key concepts in your research question clearly and consistently. Ensure definitions are:

- Internally consistent across all testing
- Piloted before full deployment
- Documented (you may draw on existing definitions from the literature)

#### 2.3 Developing the Research Question

Start at a high level. As operational definitions develop, subsidiary questions will emerge — these help narrow focus to the precise area of investigation.

### Step 3: Literature Review

#### 3.1 What Is a Literature Review?

A literature review involves:

1. Background reading — survey what has already been done
2. Search reading — compile a list of sources
3. Read and evaluate what you have found
4. Structure and produce the review

#### 3.2 Why Is It Necessary?

- Find out what is already known
- Understand the different approaches taken
- Compare different opinions and findings
- Learn the vocabulary of the field
- Identify the important unresolved issues

#### 3.3 What Counts as Literature?

- Academic books and journal articles
- Media and news sources
- 'Grey' literature — produced by and for organisations (policy papers, reports)
- Official documents (government publications, institutional data)
- Search engine results (with appropriate critical scrutiny)

#### 3.4 Critical Analysis

Critical analysis means asking how, why, and whether an argument holds up. When reading, interrogate both the source and your own purpose:

| Ask of the source | Ask of yourself |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| Why did you write this? | Why am I reading this? |
| Who did you write it for? | Was it written for me? |
| What was your purpose? | What am I looking for? |
| What questions were you asking? | What questions am I asking? |
| What answers did you find? | Do I find the answers credible? |
| What is your evidence? | Do I accept the evidence? |
| What is your conclusion? | Do I agree with the conclusions? |

**Checklist for evaluating a paper:**

- Are the aims and objectives clear?
- Are hypotheses, research questions, and underlying theories explained?
- Is funding or conflict of interest disclosed?
- Are operational definitions clearly stated?
- Is the methodology appropriate and clearly described?
- Are ethical issues addressed?
- Is the sampling process explained?
- Are the limitations of the methods discussed?
- Are results clearly presented and discussed?
- Is the conclusion clear and generalisable?
- Are implications for policy or practice discussed?

#### 3.5 Searching for Literature

- Search using keywords — a spider diagram can help map all related areas
- Consider the type of source needed (peer-reviewed, grey literature, etc.)
- Plan your search strategy in advance

#### 3.6 Reading the Literature

**Before reading:**

- Skim structure, headings, and abstract
- Read any introduction or contents page
- Ask: *Why am I reading this?*
- Decide which sections are relevant

**While reading:**

- Do a quick pass, reading opening sentences of each paragraph
- Check: *Is this going to be useful to me?*
- Read carefully with your research question in mind
- Note page numbers of key points or quotations you may use
- Record the full reference

### Step 4: Data and Ethics

#### 4.1 The Role of Data

Data is used as a proxy to infer what happens to social phenomena.

**Forms of data:**

- Spoken and written text
- Non-verbal communication
- Factual data (through observation or knowledge of the owner)
- Value statements (opinions based on personal judgement or criteria)

**Additional considerations:**

- How is data structured? (Chronologically, thematically, contextually)
- Data is constructed by individuals or entities — consider both what the data *contains* and what it *means*
- Primary data (created by the researcher) and secondary data (produced by others) have different strengths and weaknesses

**Data–methodology alignment:**

| Paradigm | Data Type | Methods |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| Positivist | Factual and value data | Quantitative |
| Interpretivist | Language, spoken, written, non-verbal | Qualitative |

> Mixed methods approaches are increasingly common.

#### 4.2 The Role of Ethics

Ethics concerns the social and moral values involved in undertaking research. Key considerations:

- **Ethics approvals** — required in most institutional contexts
- **Informed consent** — must be freely given, with full information about risks
- **Power relationships** — between researcher and participants
- **Protection from harm** — physical, psychological, reputational; includes confidentiality, anonymity, data protection, and cultural sensitivity

### Step 5: Research Design

> Design is the skeleton (how data is structured). Strategy is the brain (how data is collected and compared). Methods are the hands (how you get to your interpretation).

Research design covers three things: how the data is structured, how to collect it, and the ways to interpret it.

#### 5.1 How Can You Use Data?

Two fundamental purposes:

1. Look for **similarities and differences**
2. Look for **relationships**

#### 5.2 Four Types of Research Design

*(How data is structured in time and space)*

| Design | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Experimental** | Cases are manipulated by the researcher to make comparisons. Typically involves experimental and control groups (e.g. RCTs). |
| **Cross-sectional** | Captures units at a single point in time. |
| **Longitudinal** | Tracks entities across a period of time. |
| **Case study** | In-depth study of a single or small number of cases. Types: critical, extreme/unique, representative/typical, revelatory. |

#### 5.3 Research Strategies

*(How data is collected and compared)*

| Strategy | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Comparative** | Makes comparisons between entities |
| **Evaluation** | Assesses value relative to defined criteria |
| **Ethnography** | Data collected through immersion in the field |
| **Grounded theory** | Collects data first, then theorises from it |

**Sampling**

Sampling is the selection of cases from a larger population. Techniques range from probability-based (random, stratified, cluster) to non-probabilistic (purposive, snowball, convenience).

Key concepts:

- **Stratification** — making the sample more representative of the population, thereby reducing sampling error
- **Probabilistic sampling** — used when the goal is generalisation to a wider population
- **Non-probabilistic sampling** — used when the goal is exploration or theory development, not statistical generalisation

#### 5.4 Research Methods

Methods should always be determined by the research questions.

**Qualitative vs. Quantitative:**

| Dimension | Qualitative | Quantitative |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| Purpose | Explore meanings, interpretations | Measure, test, generalise |
| Data | Words, observations, narratives | Numbers, statistics |
| Paradigm fit | Interpretivist | Positivist |
| Output | Themes, theory | Relationships, significance |

**Ways of combining methods:**

1. **Mixed methods** — triangulate different data types to address the same questions
2. **Qual → Quant** — qualitative phase used to inform quantitative design
3. **Quant → Qual** — qualitative phase used to explain interesting quantitative findings

#### 5.5 Analysis and Interpretation

Stems from the research paradigm. The paradigm determines what counts as valid evidence and what kinds of claims the analysis can support.

| Method | Type |
|--------|------|
| Statistical analysis | Quantitative |
| Thematic analysis | Qualitative |
| Narrative analysis | Qualitative |
| Discourse analysis | Qualitative |
| Content analysis | Both |
| Grounded theory | Qualitative |

> Analysis tells you *what* the data shows. Interpretation tells you *what it means* — the claims it licenses and their significance for the research question.

---

### Step 6: Conclusion

The content of the conclusion should reflect the research project. It should address:

1. **Summary** — a concise overview of the work undertaken
2. **Research question** — has it been answered? What has been learnt?
3. **Evaluation** — how successful were the results?
4. **Strengths and weaknesses** — what worked, what didn't, and what would you change?
5. **Benefits** — who benefits from this research and in what way?
6. **Practical application** — what suggestions can be made for practice or policy?
7. **Future directions** — what still needs to be done?

## 5. Other Things to Consider

### Planning the Project

Two groups of questions:

**Research questions:**

- What is the purpose of the research?
- What research has already been done?
- How will you collect, analyse, and present findings?
- What do you think the outcome of the work will be?

**Practicalities:**

- Working alone or as part of a team?
- What is the timetable?
- How will you measure progress?

---

### The Importance of Audience

**Who:**

- Know your audience
- Try to understand how they think
- How they view the topic
- What their experiences are
- How would they react

**What:**

- What would they want to hear, and what would they not?
- Are they a homogenous group? Do you need to meet multiple needs?

**Why:**

- Why do they want it?

**How:**

- How much detail is required?

**Clarity:**

- Think about reading level and choice of wording
- Consider the reader's prior knowledge and baseline understanding

---

### Writing for Research

**Process:**

1. **Think** — define, explore ideas, generate questions
2. **Organise** — structure the ideas
3. **Write** — attend to grammar, word choice, numbers, spelling, presentation, and verb tenses; write the first draft by breaking it down into questions and thinking about how each sentence answers them
4. **Review**
5. **Publish**

---

### Writing for Reports

Reports should be:

- Brief — make it succinct
- Clear
- Precise — say what needs to be said, no more
- Simple
- Well structured
- Descriptive
- Planned

**Structure:**

- **Beginning** — where are we now?
- **Middle** — current findings, methods, review; different possibilities for the future
- **End** — conclusion and recommendation; present proof of how you reached your conclusions; anticipate objections

---

### Data Presentation

Think about the form of data that makes findings easiest to understand:

- Tables
- Graphs (line, pie, bar)
- Summary statistics (averages, percentages, etc.)

---

### Presenting

1. Tell the audience what you are going to tell them
2. Tell them
3. Tell them what you have told them
